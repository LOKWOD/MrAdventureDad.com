The 20-Minute Family Claim Check: One Headline, Three Questions, No Sharing

The goal is not to win an argument or teach a child that everything is fake. It is to say what a source actually supports—and to leave a gap labeled unknown.

Use a calm example: Suggested ages 8–12 with an adult reading and operating the browser; this is our teaching range, not an independently validated curriculum. No account, social feed, download, public comment or posting is required. Start with an ordinary opening-hours claim or the invented case below, not graphic news, health rumors, emergencies or a frightening family dispute.

Three questions before the reaction

Write three headings on scrap paper: Who is behind it? What evidence is shown? When and where does it apply? These questions do different jobs. Identifying the author does not prove the statement, and finding an old true statement does not make it current. A familiar logo is a lead to investigate, not the finish line.

The Digital Inquiry Group describes lateral reading: leaving an unfamiliar page to find what other sources establish about the organization behind it. We adapt that source-checking idea to a short family session. Our three-card routine and fictional case are original, not a copied worksheet, certification or promise that twenty minutes makes a child misinformation-proof.

What you need—and what you do not

Use scrap paper, a pencil and, optionally, one adult-controlled browser. Have water and a normal snack before opening the laptop; keep drinks away from it. Use the restroom first so the session has a clean start. No subscription, app installation, browser extension, AI account or paid course is needed.

The adult chooses and previews every page. Avoid open comment sections, autoplay feeds, advertisements that look like download buttons and searches that could expose upsetting material. Keep personal names, school information, passwords and location details out of queries. A child can ask the questions and read short passages while the adult controls navigation. Staying offline is a complete option, not a lesser version.

The 20-minute session card

Pause, trace, qualify

0–4 minutes: isolate one claim

Write the exact practical assertion in one sentence. Separate it from opinion, jokes, advertising and how it makes you feel.

4–9: identify the source

Find the named organization or author and who controls the page. Leave the page when needed to investigate that organization.

9–15: follow the evidence

Open the original record or responsible organization. Check its date, location and scope. Look for independent corroboration when the claim needs it.

15–20: write a bounded answer

State supported, contradicted or not established by what we checked. Add the strongest source and one remaining uncertainty. Close without sharing.

Good ending: “We do not know yet” is allowed. Reaching the timer does not convert an unresolved claim into a fact.

A fictional practice case with an answer key

Everything in this paragraph is invented for practice. A card says: “The town museum is free every Saturday.” A second card, labeled “Museum visitor notice, revised October 1, 2026,” says: “General admission is free on the first Saturday of each month. Special ticketed programs are excluded.” There is no real named museum, verified price or event recommendation in this example.

Ask the child to underline the claim, identify who would own the admission policy and circle the words that narrow the promise. The answer is not simply “fake.” The first card overstates frequency and omits exclusions. A supported rewrite for the fictional notice is: “The notice says general admission is free on the first Saturday; separately ticketed programs are excluded.” A real trip would still require checking that the notice is current for the intended date.

Then remove the second card. Without it, the first assertion is not established by the material supplied. It has not automatically become false. This small change teaches a useful boundary: lack of evidence in your hand is not proof that an event never happened or a policy never existed.

Do not count copied pages as independent witnesses

Two websites can repeat the same press release, quotation or social post. Ask where each obtained the information. If both point to one notice, you have two routes to one source, not two independent confirmations. A copied sentence without its original link is a reason to keep tracing, not to count louder agreement.

A responsible organization’s notice is particularly useful for its own opening hours or policy. It is not automatically an independent source for every broad claim about its own quality or success. Match expertise to the question: the museum owns its visitor rules; an unrelated shopping listing does not. For serious contested claims, this quick family routine is a starting habit, not a substitute for specialist reporting or professional advice.

Publication date, event date and place are separate

Have the child find a date and say what it labels. Was the page published then, updated then, or describing an event that happened then? A new repost can contain an old picture. A true closure in a different city cannot decide whether your local attraction opens tomorrow.

Do not assume a search-result snippet contains the whole qualification. Open the actual page with the adult and read the relevant surrounding paragraph. Save a short note with the source title, URL and check date if another outing depends on it. Do not copy an entire article or preserve private messages as a family exercise.

Pictures and AI answers are leads, not automatic proof

A convincing image may be old, cropped, staged, altered or generated. A short caption may name the wrong place. If you cannot trace the original context confidently, write that limitation. Do not ask a child to identify a real person, diagnose a scene or pronounce a picture authentic from appearance alone.

An AI summary or search answer can suggest a source to inspect, but a fluent sentence is not the original evidence. Open its cited record if available and check whether the record supports the exact claim. If the link is missing, broken or irrelevant, leave the assertion unresolved. This is a checking task, not a competition to catch the machine or a relative making a mistake.

Use a four-line evidence note

  1. Claim: one sentence, with the date and place it concerns.
  2. Best source checked: responsible organization or original record, plus check date.
  3. What it supports: the narrow statement actually established.
  4. What is still unknown: missing date, inaccessible record, conflicting account or a question outside our knowledge.

Keep this on paper at home. Do not publish a child’s work, contact a stranger, submit a form, confront a claimant or leave a corrective comment as part of the lesson. A family can learn careful wording without becoming a public fact-checking account. Correct your own sentence first.

Five-minute, offline and low-energy options

Five minutes: use only the two fictional museum cards. Find “every” versus “first” and stop. Offline: the adult writes a second invented notice with an obvious date or location mismatch and labels every card fictional. Use large print, read aloud or let a child dictate the evidence note.

Low energy or distress: close the device, put the paper away and return another day. This should not delay dinner, bedtime or reassurance. If the family wants connection rather than analysis, use the living-room picnic or one-on-one plan. Bad weather changes nothing about the indoor setup; unsafe travel is not needed to find an example.

Who should skip this version

Skip when an adult cannot supervise browsing, a child is distressed by the subject, the example contains personal accusations or the goal has become winning a political argument. Health, legal, financial and immediate safety decisions belong with appropriate authoritative guidance, not a child’s twenty-minute verdict. Never wait on this activity in an emergency.

Keep the lesson distinct from collecting family memories: the oral-history guide includes consent and uncertainty in personal recollection. The library-night plan can provide a quieter follow-up with a librarian’s help. The calendar reset helps choose a rested learning window.
