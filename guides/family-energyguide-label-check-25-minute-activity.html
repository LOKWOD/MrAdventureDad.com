The 25-Minute Family EnergyGuide Label Check: Compare One Appliance, Buy Nothing

The big yellow number is not your future bill. Read the assumptions, compare like with like, write one evidence sentence, and stop before the activity becomes appliance shopping.

No-spend setup: use one current EnergyGuide label from an appliance the household already owns, a seller or manufacturer page, or the Federal Trade Commission’s official explainer. An adult controls the device and avoids exposing account, address or utility-bill data. This lesson does not recommend replacing an appliance.

The blunt version

An EnergyGuide label tells you how much energy an appliance is estimated to use and how it compares with similar models. The annual operating cost is an estimate based on typical use and a national average energy price. Your household use and local price can differ. That difference is the whole lesson.

This 25-minute activity is for school-age kids who can read a chart and do simple multiplication or comparison with help. It uses one appliance class, one label and one evidence sentence. Do not turn a math exercise into a $1,000 purchase or let a child interpret account data.

The five-field evidence card

1. What is being compared?

Record the appliance type, capacity or feature group shown on the label. A refrigerator cannot be compared fairly with every other refrigerator regardless of size and configuration.

2. Estimated energy use

Copy the label’s energy-consumption figure and unit exactly. Do not convert units until the child can explain what was printed.

3. Estimated annual cost

Copy the estimate, then write “not our bill.” Find the energy-price assumption or official explanation that supports the estimate.

4. Comparison range

Mark where the model sits among similar models shown on that label. Explain that the endpoints belong to the displayed comparison class, not every appliance ever made.

5. Separate marks

Note whether an ENERGY STAR logo appears, but do not treat EnergyGuide and ENERGY STAR as the same program. One is a required comparison label for covered products; the other identifies qualifying efficient models.

Evidence sentence: “For this appliance class and label assumptions, the model is estimated to use ___ per year and sits ___ in the displayed comparison range; our actual cost can differ.”

Supplies and age fit

You need a current label image from an official or seller source, one index card or sheet of paper, pencil and calculator. The FTC says EnergyGuide labels appear on many covered appliances, including refrigerators, dishwashers, clothes washers, freezers, water heaters, televisions and room or portable air conditioners. Not every product has one.

Ages 7–9 can find the yellow label, units and range with an adult. Ages 10–12 can explain the cost assumption and write the evidence sentence. Teens can compare two genuinely similar models, but the 25-minute version still stops before shopping.

The 25-minute plan

  1. Minutes 0–3—pick one class: choose a refrigerator, dishwasher or other covered category. Use a safe visible label or official online example. Do not move, unplug or climb behind an appliance.
  2. Minutes 3–7—circle the identity: identify appliance type, model reference if public and relevant, capacity or comparison group. Hide any household account information that appears elsewhere on the screen.
  3. Minutes 7–12—copy the two estimates: write the annual energy-use number with its unit and the estimated annual operating cost. Add the words “estimate” and “label assumption.”
  4. Minutes 12–16—read the range: find the low and high endpoints for similar models and mark the displayed model’s position. Ask what must stay similar for the comparison to be useful.
  5. Minutes 16–20—separate the programs: read the FTC explanation of EnergyGuide, then check the official ENERGY STAR definition. Write one difference without claiming either mark guarantees a household’s bill.
  6. Minutes 20–23—make one what-if: if the label provides an energy-price assumption, ask what happens to estimated cost when the price rises or falls. Keep it proportional and label the result a scenario, not a forecast.
  7. Minutes 23–25—write and stop: complete the evidence sentence, date the source and put the card away. No shopping tab is required.

Energy use is not operating cost

Energy use is a quantity such as kilowatt-hours per year. Operating cost applies a price assumption to that use. A child may multiply the energy-use estimate by a clearly stated price to understand the relationship, but the result remains a scenario. Local rates can include tiers, fixed charges, time-of-use pricing, taxes and other terms beyond the lesson.

Do not copy the household utility bill into a child’s worksheet. If an adult chooses to use a current local energy price, remove account identifiers and explain which charge is being used. A bill total divided by kilowatt-hours may mix fixed and variable costs; it is not automatically the label’s intended comparison price.

EnergyGuide is not ENERGY STAR

The FTC says EnergyGuide helps shoppers understand energy use and compare similar appliances. ENERGY STAR is a separate program administered by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency; products must meet program efficiency guidelines to use its mark. A product can have an EnergyGuide label without carrying ENERGY STAR.

Do not tell children that a yellow label means “efficient,” that ENERGY STAR predicts a specific savings amount, or that the lowest annual cost is automatically the right appliance. Size, installation, ventilation, performance, water use, repair history, noise, purchase price and household need can also matter. The FTC specifically tells shoppers to measure the space and allow door and ventilation clearance.

The like-for-like gate

Before comparing two labels, answer four questions: Are they the same appliance type? Are capacity and configuration comparable? Do the labels use the same units and relevant assumptions? Are both labels current and legible? If any answer is no, stop at reading one label.

This gate is the authority-building value of the exercise. It prevents a child—and an adult—from treating every prominent number as comparable. Use the same habit in the grocery receipt detective, where unit price and package size also need context, and the recipe scaling lab, where units have to remain attached to numbers.

Common mistakes to catch

  • Calling estimated annual cost an exact forecast.
  • Comparing different sizes or configurations because both are refrigerators.
  • Dropping “per year,” kilowatt-hours or another unit while copying.
  • Assuming the left end of a range means best overall product.
  • Treating EnergyGuide and ENERGY STAR as interchangeable.
  • Using an old marketplace image with an unreadable or mismatched model number.
  • Entering an address, account number or full utility bill into an online calculator.
  • Starting a replacement purchase before the current appliance’s condition, installation and household need are assessed by the responsible adult.

Safety and privacy boundaries

Do not pull out a refrigerator, open an electrical panel, remove an appliance cover, disconnect gas, touch wiring or climb to find a label. Use an online label or the FTC sample when the physical label is not safely visible. Repairs, gas, electrical work, ventilation and installation belong with the exact manufacturer instructions and qualified professionals.

An adult owns the browser, purchases and any utility account. Children do not need a home address, service identifier, account number, meter number or payment history to learn this concept. Crop screenshots to the public product label. Delete accidental private captures according to the household’s normal device practice.

Bad-weather, low-energy and no-label versions

Bad weather: this is already an indoor plan; stop if power conditions make device use unsafe. Low energy: read one FTC sample and complete fields two and three in 10 minutes. No printer: use paper beside the adult’s screen. No safe physical label: use an official manufacturer or FTC example. No EnergyGuide for that product: choose a covered appliance rather than inventing fields. Math frustration: skip the what-if and finish the evidence sentence orally.

Who should skip it

Skip this activity if the only available source exposes private account data, if reaching the label requires moving an appliance, or if the family is in the middle of an urgent repair. Skip comparison when labels are for different classes. A child who needs a more concrete measurement activity may prefer the room measure-and-draw plan.
