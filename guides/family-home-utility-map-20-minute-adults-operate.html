The 20-Minute Family Home-Utility Map: Kids Point, Adults Operate

Kids learn where to go and who to tell. Adults identify the controls. Nobody practices on gas, live electrical equipment or damaged utilities.

Safety boundary: this is a location-and-communication map, not a utility shutoff drill. Children point, remember exits and tell an adult. Adults do not operate gas or electrical controls merely for practice. If you smell gas, hear hissing, see sparks, find damaged wires or see water near electrical equipment, leave the area, keep others away and contact the utility or emergency services from a safe place. Only the utility or a qualified professional should restore gas service.

The blunt version

Families often know where the flashlight is and still cannot answer the useful questions: Which exit avoids the utility room? Where is the water shutoff? What number is on the electric bill? Which adult is allowed to touch what? Fix that on one sheet of paper without turning anything off.

This takes 20 minutes, costs nothing and exposes no address online. Draw a rough home outline, mark safe observation points and write the contacts adults need. Store one copy near the family emergency information and one secure digital copy if that fits the household. Do not photograph account numbers, meter numbers, keys or access codes for a public post.

The four-color map

Blue: water

Mark where an adult can observe the main water shutoff and any required tool. Do not turn it for the lesson. Note the plumber or building contact and the safest route that avoids standing water.

Yellow: gas or fuel

Mark the outdoor safe direction to leave—not a child operating point. Write the utility emergency number. Never use a flame, switch, phone or tool in the suspected leak area.

Red: electrical

Mark the panel location for adults and a keep-away boundary for children. Never approach a wet, sparking, hot, buzzing or damaged panel. Your electrician and utility instructions control.

Green: exits and meeting place

Mark two usable exits from sleeping areas and one outdoor family meeting place. This is the child’s job: get out, go to the meeting place and tell an adult.

Minute-by-minute

  1. Minutes 0–3: adult draws a rough outline with no address, account number or security detail.
  2. Minutes 3–7: walk the normal path to each utility area without touching equipment. Stop if the route is cluttered, wet or unsafe.
  3. Minutes 7–11: adult marks the water, gas/fuel and electrical locations. Child marks exits and the meeting place.
  4. Minutes 11–15: write utility emergency, landlord/building, plumber, electrician and out-of-area family contacts as applicable.
  5. Minutes 15–18: each child points to the exit and says: “I leave, meet outside and tell an adult.”
  6. Minutes 18–20: adult stores the map, moves clutter away from the safe path and schedules any professional question that the walk exposed.

The operating rule

Write this at the top: Kids point. Adults decide. Utilities or qualified professionals restore. FEMA preparedness guidance says household members should know how and when utilities may be turned off, but the home, utility and equipment instructions matter. The American Red Cross warns that gas should be turned off only when a leak is suspected or authorities instruct it and that a professional must turn it back on.

That distinction prevents a planning activity from becoming amateur electrical or gas work. Renters should use the landlord or building emergency process. Families with solar, batteries, generators, wells, septic pumps, medical equipment or automatic transfer equipment need the specific manufacturer, utility and qualified-technician plan—not a generic internet sequence.

Gas or fuel: leave first

If there is a gas odor, hissing, visible damage or suspected leak, do not hunt for the meter. Do not operate lights, appliances, phones, garage doors or vehicles in the area. Move everyone to a safe place and follow the utility’s emergency instructions. The family map should show the meeting place and phone number, not teach a child to use a wrench.

For propane, heating oil or other fuel, use the supplier’s written emergency guidance. Do not transfer generic natural-gas instructions to a different system.

Electrical: dry, intact and adult-only

The map may mark the panel’s location. It does not authorize opening covers, removing dead fronts, identifying conductors or touching damaged equipment. If there is water near the panel, buzzing, arcing, heat, smoke or a burning smell, stay away and call for help. Keep the access path clear without storing toys, paper or household chemicals in the work space.

Pair the location map with the family fire-escape drill, which handles exit behavior, and the power-outage drill, which handles lights, food, phones and carbon-monoxide boundaries. Those are separate skills.

Water: locate, do not improvise

Adults can identify the main valve and note whether a special tool or building staff is required. Do not force a stuck valve, operate an unlabeled control or enter a flooded area. A plumber can label the correct valve, check its condition and explain the home-specific process. Children only need to know: move away from water near electricity and tell an adult.

Medical and accessibility layer

Households using refrigerated medicine, powered medical equipment, lifts or accessibility devices need a written provider and power-outage plan. Add the provider, utility medical-notification program and backup contacts if applicable. Do not publish diagnoses or account details on the visible family map. Use the family medicine plan for the go-bag side and the emergency contact-card drill for the child’s information.

What to include

  • Rough room outline and two exit paths.
  • Outdoor meeting place.
  • Adult-only utility locations and keep-away areas.
  • Utility emergency numbers and building contact.
  • Plumber and electrician contact routes.
  • Flashlight location and one backup light.
  • Date checked and next six-month review date.

What to leave off

  • Public-facing address, account or meter numbers.
  • Alarm codes, lockbox locations or hidden keys.
  • A child’s bedroom label or daily schedule.
  • Unverified shutoff instructions copied from a different home.
  • A promise that one valve or switch controls every system.

Low-energy and renter versions

The five-minute version marks only two exits, the meeting place, the flashlight and the building emergency number. That is enough for a tired school night. Renters should map the apartment exits, meeting place, panel location only if safely accessible, and the landlord or superintendent contact. Never enter a locked mechanical room or defeat a building restriction.

Kid-meltdown backup

If a child gets anxious, stop the utility tour. Switch to the ordinary contact-card exercise or let the child draw only the outdoor meeting place. Do not stage darkness, alarms, gas smells or scary scenarios. Competence grows from a calm, repeatable script: leave, meet, tell.

Who should skip the walk-through

Skip any utility area that is wet, damaged, hot, sparking, cramped, locked, under construction or otherwise unsafe. Skip the whole activity during an actual emergency. Families who cannot safely reach or identify controls should ask the landlord, utility or qualified professional to document the home-specific plan.
